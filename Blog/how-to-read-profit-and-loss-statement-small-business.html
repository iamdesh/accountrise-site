First, the simple definition

A profit and loss statement (also called an income statement or P&L) summarizes income and expenses for a period. It helps answer: Did the business earn a profit, where did the money go in the income statement, and what changed compared with the prior period? It does not, on its own, tell you how much cash is in the bank.

You do not need to be an accountant to get useful information from your P&L. You do need consistent categories, a period that makes sense, and books that have been reviewed. Use this guide as a practical starting point—not as a substitute for the context behind your own numbers.

01 · Read it top to bottom

What the main lines on a P&L actually mean

Most small-business profit and loss reports follow a flow: income at the top, direct costs next, then overhead and other items. Exact labels vary by accounting system, industry, and report settings, so focus on what each group represents.

Common P&L sections and the question each one helps answer
Line or sectionWhat it representsQuestion to ask
Revenue / incomeSales and other income included in the report.Is sales growth real and repeatable?
Cost of goods sold (COGS)Direct costs assigned to goods or services sold.Are direct costs rising faster than revenue?
Gross profitRevenue minus COGS.How much remains before overhead?
Operating expensesOverhead such as rent, software, insurance, and administrative payroll.Which costs changed or need context?
Operating incomeGross profit less the operating expenses included above this line.Are core operations generating a surplus?
Other income and expensesItems shown below operating income, depending on the report.Are non-core items affecting the result?
Net income / net profitThe final result after the income and expense items included on the statement.What is the bottom-line result for this period?

Important: COGS treatment depends on the business and accounting setup. For a service company, direct labor or contractor costs may be classified as direct costs; for a retailer or manufacturer, inventory and production costing matter. Review the account mapping rather than relying on the account name alone.

04 · Look for change, not just size

How to review a P&L without getting lost in the detail

The number by itself is only part of the story. A more useful review asks whether it is accurate, what changed, why it changed, and whether the change is expected to continue.

Compare three views

  • This month versus last month: useful for spotting sudden changes, seasonality, and timing differences.
  • This month versus the same period last year: useful when the business is seasonal or has a different sales cycle across the year.
  • Actual versus budget or plan: useful for checking whether results match the assumptions behind hiring, pricing, inventory, and spending decisions.

Investigate meaningful variances

Start with the lines that moved the most in dollars or percentage terms, then investigate the explanation. A higher software bill might be an annual renewal; a lower expense might reflect a missing bill rather than an efficiency improvement. Document the reason and any follow-up action.

A useful review question: “What changed, what evidence supports the explanation, and does anything need to be corrected or acted on?” Avoid changing an account merely to make the report look better.

05 · Avoid false conclusions

Six common P&L mistakes that can mislead owners

01 Treating profit as cash

Revenue may be earned before a customer pays, and loan principal or equipment purchases may use cash without appearing as regular P&L expenses in the same way.

02 Reviewing unreconciled books

Missing transactions, duplicate entries, and unreconciled bank activity can distort the report. Reconcile and review before relying on the numbers.

03 Misclassifying direct costs

Putting direct costs in overhead—or overhead in direct costs—can distort gross margin and make comparisons inconsistent.

04 Ignoring timing adjustments

Accruals, prepayments, inventory movements, depreciation, and other period-end items can affect which period shows an expense or income.

05 Comparing different periods

Compare like-for-like periods and check whether the report uses consistent dates, accounting basis, filters, and account groupings.

06 Assuming every margin has one target

Healthy margins vary by industry, business model, pricing, and stage of growth. Use relevant context and your own trend—not a generic online benchmark.

07 · Keep the reports in context

Why a positive P&L does not guarantee healthy cash flow

Your P&L measures results over a period under your accounting method. Your bank balance reflects cash received and paid. The two can move differently for several reasons:

  • Accounts receivable: you may have recorded revenue for invoices customers have not paid yet.
  • Inventory and other working capital: cash may be tied up in stock or balances before the related expense fully reaches the P&L.
  • Loan principal: principal repayment uses cash but generally is not a P&L expense; interest is treated separately.
  • Equipment and other capital purchases: cash may be paid upfront while the cost is recognized over time through depreciation, where applicable.
  • Owner distributions: these can reduce cash without being operating expenses.

Use your P&L with the balance sheet and cash flow information. If profit is increasing but cash is shrinking, review collections, vendor timing, inventory, debt service, and large purchases rather than assuming the P&L alone explains the problem.

08 · Get the books decision-ready

When to bring in bookkeeping or accounting support

Consider getting help when reports change unexpectedly every month, bank reconciliations remain unfinished, direct costs are unclear, old balances accumulate, or you cannot explain significant variances. The goal is not simply to produce a report—it is to make the numbers consistent enough to help you run the business.

A bookkeeper or accounting professional can organize transactions, reconcile accounts, review classifications, support month-end close, and prepare recurring reports. Ask what is included, what remains your responsibility, how review issues are escalated, and how your CPA or tax adviser will receive the final information.

Questions owners ask

Profit and loss statement FAQs

What is a profit and loss statement?

A profit and loss statement, also called an income statement or P&L, summarizes income and expenses over a selected period and shows the resulting profit or loss under the accounting method and classifications used.

How often should a small business review its P&L?

Many small businesses review a P&L monthly after key reconciliations and adjustments are complete. Businesses with fast-moving operations may also use weekly indicators, while retaining a consistent monthly close process.

Does a positive P&L mean I have cash in the bank?

No. A P&L reports income and expenses, not the full movement in cash. Uncollected invoices, inventory purchases, loan principal payments, capital purchases, and owner distributions can cause cash to differ from reported profit.

What is the difference between gross profit and net profit?

Gross profit is revenue minus cost of goods sold or direct costs included in that line. Net profit is the result after the other expenses and income items included in the statement, which may include operating expenses, interest, taxes, and other items depending on how the report is presented.

Can bookkeeping support help me understand my P&L?

A bookkeeping or accounting professional can help reconcile accounts, review classifications, explain month-over-month changes, and prepare consistent reports. Tax, assurance, and other regulated advice should be handled by the appropriately qualified professional.
